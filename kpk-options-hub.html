4 · The basics, in plain language

For anyone on the team who does not live in options land.
What does “subscribing an option” actually mean?

It means selling insurance on a price move, and being paid a premium upfront for it. A covered call: the treasury holds ETH and sells someone the right to buy that ETH at a fixed higher price (the strike) on a fixed date. If ETH stays below the strike, we keep the ETH and the premium. If it rises above, we sell at the strike — we still keep the premium, but give up the upside beyond the strike.

A cash-secured put is the mirror: we hold cash and sell someone the right to sell us ETH at a fixed lower price. We keep the premium either way; if the price falls below the strike we end up buying ETH at the strike — more expensive than the market at that point, so it only makes sense at a strike where we would be happy accumulating.

What is implied volatility, and why does the hub convert everything into it?

An option's price mostly reflects how much movement the market expects — that expectation, expressed as an annualised percentage, is implied volatility (IV). Converting every quote into IV puts a 3-week quote, a 3-month quote, a % premium and an APR all in the same unit, so they can be compared with each other and with the live listed market. A premium that looks generous in % terms can be stingy in vol terms, and vice versa.

How does the hub decide a verdict?

Three tests, in order. First: is the quote's IV above the asset's recent realised volatility? Selling movement for less than the asset actually moves is a losing trade regardless of the headline yield — quotes below realised are blocked. Second: is the quote at least 3 vol points above the listed bid — what Deribit would pay us for the identical option right now? Less than that and the listed market is the better counterparty. Third: anything between the listed bid and roughly fair value is negotiable — defensible if the provider brings real operational advantages (no margin management, on-chain settlement, size).

What are the risks — what can go wrong?

Covered call: the downside of the asset is still fully ours — the premium is a small cushion, not protection. And if the price flies through the strike, the upside beyond it belongs to the buyer; that regret is uncapped, which is why strike choice matters more than premium size. Cash-secured put: if the market crashes we buy at the strike, above the new market price. The right frame: only sell puts at prices where accumulating would genuinely be welcome. Both: premium is compensation for risk, never free yield. If a yield looks too good, the volatility being sold is being underpriced somewhere.

Why compare against the listed bid and not the mid?

The mid is where nobody trades. If we sell, we sell at the bid — so the bid is the honest benchmark for what a provider must beat. Rule inherited from the July research: when you must sell, use the bid; when you must buy, the ask; never the mark.

When is it simply the wrong time to subscribe?

Two red flags. Implied below realised — sellers are being underpaid for actual movement; wait. Very low vol percentile — premiums are thin in absolute terms; capping upside for a small premium right before a vol expansion is the classic treasury mistake. The best moments to subscribe combine IV above realised and a decent absolute vol level, at strikes the treasury is happy to be exercised at.

Method. Quotes are converted with Black-76 on the Deribit forward for the matched expiry, r = 0, bisection to 10⁻⁶ — the same conventions validated in the July 2026 options research (validate.py reproduces the published BTC 36.35% / ETH 53.9% anchors). Fair value is interpolated on the listed surface: linear in strike on the out-of-the-money side, linear in total variance across expiries.
